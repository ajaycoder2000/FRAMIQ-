Grape heat stress: the signs to watch

Vines tell you they are struggling well before the fruit shows it. Here is what to look for, and what the forecast should trigger.

Grapevines tolerate heat better than most crops — but "tolerates" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Sustained heat during ripening changes sugar-acid balance, and a single extreme afternoon can sunburn exposed clusters permanently.

The thresholds that matter

Vine photosynthesis starts declining above roughly 30 °C (86 °F) and largely shuts down above 35 °C (95 °F). The vine isn't dying at that point — it's idling. Sustained idling during ripening is what costs you.

What matters more than any single day is consecutive days above threshold, and whether nights cool down. Vines recover overnight. If the low stays above about 20 °C (68 °F) for several nights, stress compounds even without a hotter daytime peak.

What the canopy is telling you

  • Leaves folding along the midrib — the earliest visible signal, often mid-afternoon. The vine is reducing exposed surface area.
  • Dull, greyish leaf colour replacing glossy green — reduced turgor.
  • Tendrils drying back at the tips — the vine is abandoning growth to protect what it has.
  • Shoot tips stalling — growth has stopped, energy is going to survival.
  • Basal leaf yellowing and drop — later-stage, the vine is shedding what it can no longer support.

Cluster sunburn is the one you cannot undo

Leaf stress recovers. Sunburned fruit does not. Damage shows as tan or brown patches on the sun-exposed side, and severely affected berries shrivel.

The risk is highest on west-facing fruit in the afternoon, and it is dramatically worse on clusters that were recently exposed. This is the trap in leaf removal: pulling leaves for airflow right before a heat spike removes the shade those clusters had adapted under. If a heat event is in the forecast, delay leaf removal until after it passes.

The forecast trigger

Three or more days forecast above 32 °C (90 °F) is the point to change your plan: irrigate ahead of the event rather than during it, postpone any leaf pulling, and check west-facing fruit after it breaks.

Irrigate before, not during

The instinct is to irrigate when the heat arrives. That's late. A vine entering a heat event with adequate soil moisture handles it far better than one you start watering on day two — water takes time to move into the root zone and into the plant.

Deficit irrigation is a legitimate strategy pre-veraison, and many growers use it deliberately to concentrate fruit. But a deliberate deficit going into a forecast heat spike is a different proposition from a deficit during mild weather. Know which one you're running.

What not to read as heat stress

Midday leaf wilting that recovers by evening is normal on hot days — the vine is regulating, not failing. Judge stress by whether the canopy has recovered by the following morning. Persistent morning wilt is the signal that matters.
